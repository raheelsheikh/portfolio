01

Overview

VCM is Saudi Arabia's Voluntary Carbon Market — a platform that allows organizations to buy and sell verified carbon credits as part of their climate commitments. It sits at the intersection of finance, environmental policy, and international trade.

The challenge with carbon markets is not the trading itself — it is that most people, including many of the organizations that need to participate, do not fully understand what a carbon credit is, how it is priced, or why it matters. The design job was to make a complex financial and environmental system understandable and accessible without making it feel simplified or patronizing to the institutional buyers and government bodies who were the primary audience.

I was the UI designer on a team that also included researchers and a project lead. The research was done collectively. The visual design, component structure, bilingual layout, and all UI decisions were my responsibility. The project ran across two phases — a soft launch first, then a full platform launch — over four months in 2022 and 2023.

What VCM does
Operates Saudi Arabia's voluntary carbon credit market, enabling organizations to offset emissions by purchasing verified carbon reduction projects
Why the design mattered
Carbon markets only work if participants trust them. A confusing or opaque interface erodes that trust before a single trade happens
The starting point
A new build from scratch. No existing platform to reference or improve — we were designing the first version of something that had never existed in this form before
02

The
Challenge

Building the first version of a financial platform in a specialist market has a very specific set of problems. You cannot rely on existing user habits because the product does not exist yet. You cannot benchmark directly because few comparable platforms exist in the region. And you are designing for an audience that spans from sophisticated institutional traders to government officials who may be encountering voluntary carbon markets for the first time.

The biggest risk in a market platform is not bad design. It is design that makes users uncertain about what they are doing. Uncertainty kills participation.

Making carbon markets understandable without dumbing them down
Carbon credits, verification standards, offset methodologies, auction mechanics — none of this is simple. The design had to surface the right level of information for each user type. Too simple and institutional buyers feel underserved. Too technical and new participants drop off before they engage.
Four very different user types, one platform
Carbon traders, government bodies, institutional buyers, and the general public all needed to find value in the same platform. Their information needs, technical literacy, and goals were completely different. The architecture had to serve all of them without making any one group feel like the design was built for someone else.
Bilingual at financial platform standards
A public website in English and Arabic sounds straightforward. For a financial platform operating in Saudi Arabia it is not. Financial terminology in Arabic has specific conventions. Number formatting, currency display, and data table reading direction all behave differently in RTL contexts. Both languages had to meet the standard a financial audience expects.
Building trust through design on a brand new platform
No one had used VCM before because it did not exist. Trust in a financial platform usually comes from track record and reputation. We had neither. The design itself had to carry the credibility — through visual quality, information clarity, and a tone that communicated institutional seriousness from day one.
03

Understanding
the Users

The team's research identified four distinct user types, each arriving at the platform with a different level of familiarity with carbon markets, different information needs, and different definitions of what a successful visit looked like. Designing for the average of these users would have served none of them well.

Carbon Traders
Specialist buyers and sellers with deep market knowledge. They need data density, pricing history, credit verification details, and fast access to auction mechanics. They do not need explanations.
Government Bodies
Regulatory stakeholders and policy teams. They need transparency, compliance information, market oversight data, and clear reporting. Trust and official positioning matter more than speed.
Institutional Buyers
Large organizations buying carbon credits for ESG compliance or sustainability commitments. They need credibility signals, project information, and a clear buying process. They arrive with intent but often limited market knowledge.
General Public
Interested citizens, researchers, and journalists. They need context, education, and a sense of the platform's purpose and scale. They are not buyers but they shape public trust in the market.

The research also surfaced a consistent concern across all user types: they did not want to feel confused about what they were doing in a financial context. For carbon traders that meant precise data. For government bodies it meant clear compliance language. For institutional buyers it meant an obvious path from interest to transaction. For the public it meant plain-language explanations that did not condescend.

04

Design
Process

The two-phase structure was not just a launch strategy — it shaped how the design was built. Phase one had to be solid enough to establish credibility at soft launch while leaving room for the full feature set that phase two would introduce. That requires designing the full system up front, not just the first phase.

Phase One — Months 1 and 2
Soft launch — platform presence and market overview
The first phase established VCM's public presence and explained what the platform was and what it did. The design priority was credibility and clarity. Key sections included an overview of the voluntary carbon market, how VCM operated within Saudi Arabia's climate commitments, the types of credits available, and the process for registration. Visual tone was set here — everything that followed had to be consistent with the foundation built in phase one. The bilingual system was also finalized in this phase so phase two could build on it directly.
Visual System Information Architecture Bilingual Foundation Component Library
Phase Two — Months 3 and 4
Full launch — auction platform, trading tools, and data transparency
Phase two brought the full trading and auction functionality into the design. This included carbon credit listings with project details and verification information, auction mechanics and bidding interfaces, market data displays, compliance documentation, and user dashboards for different account types. The challenge in phase two was taking the clean, editorial tone established in phase one and extending it into data-heavy screens without losing the clarity that made phase one work. Dense data and visual clarity are not naturally compatible — keeping them together required careful typographic hierarchy and a consistent color system for data states.

05

Key Design
Decisions

These were not styling choices. They were structural decisions about how the platform communicated, what it prioritized, and how different users would experience the same information.

Decision 01 — Visual Tone
Financial credibility over environmental enthusiasm
Many sustainability platforms use heavy environmental imagery — forests, skies, earth tones — as their primary visual language. I chose not to. VCM is a financial market first. The people using it are making financial decisions. The visual system was built to communicate institutional credibility: clean typography, structured data layouts, a restrained green that referenced the environmental purpose without becoming a metaphor. Environmental imagery was used purposefully in context sections but never as a substitute for clear information design.
Decision 02 — Progressive Disclosure for Complex Concepts
Explaining carbon credits without interrupting experienced users
Carbon market terminology is genuinely complex. Terms like additionality, vintage year, verification standard, and retirement are standard in the industry but unfamiliar to institutional buyers and the public. I used progressive disclosure throughout — key terms linked to clear explanations that appeared contextually without breaking the main flow. Experienced traders could ignore these. New participants could access them without the interface feeling like it was designed for beginners. Both groups got what they needed from the same screen.
Decision 03 — Arabic Financial Standards
Numbers, currency, and data tables built for Arabic financial conventions
Arabic financial interfaces have specific conventions that differ from English. Number display, decimal separators, currency positioning, and table reading direction all follow different rules. More importantly, financial data tables in Arabic are not simply mirrored versions of English tables — the logic of how a user scans a row of data changes with reading direction, which affects which columns need visual emphasis. I rebuilt the data table components specifically for Arabic reading patterns rather than adapting the English versions.
Decision 04 — Transparency as a Design Principle
Showing how credits are verified, not just that they are
Trust in a carbon market depends entirely on whether participants believe the credits are real and the methodology is sound. Rather than treating verification as a footnote, I made it a visible part of every credit listing. Project origin, verification standard, auditor, vintage year, and retirement status were all surfaced clearly rather than buried in documentation. This was a deliberate choice to prioritize transparency over visual simplicity, because in this context transparency is what enables participation.
Decision 05 — Soft Launch as a Design Test
Building phase one to validate the visual system before phase two committed to it
The phased launch structure was not just a business decision — it was a design advantage. Phase one let the visual system, typography, and bilingual architecture be tested with real users on a live platform before the more complex trading screens of phase two were finalized. Feedback from the soft launch informed three component refinements in phase two, particularly in how market data was labeled and how auction status was communicated. Designing phase two after real users had seen phase one produced better work than if both phases had been designed simultaneously.

07

What I
Learned

Domain complexity is a design problem, not a content problem. Carbon markets are hard to understand. The instinct is to fix that with better copywriting. But the design itself carries as much explanatory power as the words — through what it surfaces first, what it groups together, what it labels clearly, and what it hides until the right moment. You cannot write your way out of a structural information problem.
Financial platforms need to earn trust before they earn engagement. Nobody trades on a platform they do not trust, regardless of how good the pricing is. Every visual decision — the typography quality, the data accuracy, the consistency of the interface — contributes to or erodes that trust. This made the design system work more consequential than it might seem on a less high-stakes product.
Phased launches are a design tool, not just a business strategy. Launching phase one gave us real data before phase two was finalized. That data changed three components. A design that is informed by real users performing real tasks on a live product is always better than one that is informed only by research and assumptions. When you have the option to phase, use it as a design advantage.
RTL financial interfaces require their own design logic, not just mirroring. This came up again after Sharjah Municipality, and it confirmed something I already believed: any Arabic interface that starts as an English interface and then gets adapted will have structural problems. The reading logic changes too fundamentally to be solved by flipping a layout. You have to start from Arabic and design outward.
Designing for a category you are not expert in forces better questions. I am not a carbon market specialist. That meant I had to question every assumption about what users understood and what they needed explained. That questioning produced a better information architecture than if I had assumed familiarity. Not knowing a domain deeply sometimes makes you a better designer of its products, because you ask the questions that expert insiders have stopped asking.
