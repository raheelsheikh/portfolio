01

Overview

The Dubai Police Criminal Investigation Department uses an internal portal to manage some of the most sensitive operational data in the emirate: criminal records, wanted persons, active case files, prison status, impounded vehicles, and real-time case updates across departments.

The system had been built entirely by developers — no designer was ever involved. It worked technically. But it had never been designed. Years of feature additions had turned it into a patchwork of inconsistent screens with no visual logic, no hierarchy, and no clear workflow. Officers were navigating a system that slowed them down rather than helping them work.

My job was to change that without breaking the trust of users who had been relying on it for years. This was not a consumer app where users forgive rough edges. These are law enforcement professionals who need accurate information fast, and cannot afford to misread a screen or lose time in a broken flow.

Due to the confidential nature of this project, actual screenshots of the portal and its data cannot be shown. This case study describes the design process, decisions, and outcomes without exposing any sensitive law enforcement information.
What the portal handles
Criminal records, wanted persons lists, active case management, prison status, impounded vehicle tracking, inter-department case routing
Who uses it
CID investigators, case officers, department heads, and administrative staff across Dubai Police branches
The starting point
A functional but entirely undesigned system — built by developers, never touched by a designer, and visually frozen somewhere around 2013
02

The
Problem

When developers build internal tools without design involvement, the structure of the system follows the structure of the database — not the mental model of the person using it. That is exactly what happened here. The portal was organized around how data was stored, not around how officers actually worked.

"Officers had memorized workarounds. That is never a good sign. It means the tool failed and people compensated for it with habit."

No visual hierarchy anywhere
Every piece of data on every screen had the same visual weight. A case number looked the same as a suspect name, which looked the same as an officer note. Officers had to read everything to find what they needed, every time.
Search that barely worked
Searching for a criminal record required knowing exact ID numbers. Partial name searches returned unpredictable results. Filtering across case status, date ranges, or officer assignments was either broken or non-existent.
Data scattered across modules
To get a full picture of a case — suspect record, current status, vehicle information, previous charges — an officer had to visit four or five separate screens with no connection between them and no way to view them together.
No design system, no consistency
Buttons in one module looked nothing like buttons in another. Status indicators had different colors in different screens meaning different things. Every module had been built by a different developer at a different time with no shared visual language.
Arabic RTL done incorrectly
The system was Arabic-only, but the layout logic was built left-to-right and then mirrored with CSS. Icons pointed the wrong direction. Form validation messages appeared on the wrong side. Reading flow fought the interface.
No role-based interface differences
An administrative clerk saw the same interface as a senior investigator. There was no distinction between read access, edit access, and approval access — leading to both confusion and security concerns about what people could or could not do.
03

Research
Approach

Redesigning a law enforcement tool without understanding how officers actually work is dangerous. You can easily optimize for the wrong thing. I spent the first phase entirely in research mode before touching Figma.

Access to users was restricted for security reasons, so I designed the research to work within those constraints — structured surveys, observed sessions where permitted, and deep-dive interviews with officers who volunteered. Every session was conducted in Arabic.

Structured Survey
A structured survey was distributed to active portal users across three departments. Questions targeted daily workflow friction, most-used modules, common errors made, and confidence levels with current features.
38 officers responded. 91% reported daily frustration with the search function. 78% said they kept personal notes to compensate for missing features.
Observed Sessions
Sat with six officers during live work sessions, watching how they navigated the portal without directing them. No test tasks — real work. The goal was to see what they actually did, not what they said they did.
Average of 4.2 unnecessary clicks observed per lookup task. Officers often opened multiple browser tabs to see related case data side by side.
Depth Interviews
One-hour interviews with eight officers at different seniority levels — two investigators, three case officers, two department heads, and one system administrator. Conducted in Arabic, focused on mental models and decision-making process.
Senior investigators wanted a case summary view at a glance. Junior officers prioritized clearer form guidance to avoid data entry errors.

The survey data gave us scale — it showed that the problems were not isolated complaints from a few vocal users. The observed sessions showed us what those problems looked like in practice. The interviews told us the mental models behind them and how different roles had different priorities.

The most valuable finding was not what officers struggled with. It was what they had stopped trying to do — tasks they had quietly given up on because the system made them too hard.

04

Design
Approach

The redesign had three non-negotiable principles from the start. First: do not change the data, only how it is presented. Officers trusted certain screens even when those screens were ugly — and any change that made data feel different would immediately lose their trust. Second: build an Arabic-first system, not an English system with Arabic text. Third: respect the severity of the context. This is not a productivity app. A visually heavy or playful interface would feel wrong to users who deal with serious content.

01
Define the information hierarchy
Working from research findings, I mapped every data point that appeared across the eight modules and ranked them by how frequently officers needed them and how critical they were. This hierarchy became the visual hierarchy — the most important information largest, most prominent, easiest to scan. Criminal record status, case number, and suspect name became the three things that were always visible first regardless of which module you were in.
Content Audit Priority Mapping Information Architecture
02
Build an Arabic-native design system
I started the design system from the Arabic perspective — not from English components then mirrored. Typography was set with a proper Arabic type scale using Cairo and Noto Kufi Arabic for different hierarchy levels. All spacing, alignment, and reading flow were built RTL from the ground up. Icons were audited for directionality — any icon implying direction (arrows, progress, navigation) was redrawn or replaced with directionally neutral alternatives. Every component was pressure-tested in full Arabic text strings before being finalized, because Arabic text is typically 20 to 30% longer than its English equivalent and breaks layouts that weren't designed with it in mind.
Arabic Typography RTL Component System Icon Audit Token Architecture
03
Redesign the dashboard as a command center
The existing dashboard was a list of module links. That was it. Officers arrived at the system with no sense of what needed attention. The redesigned dashboard became a genuine command center — summarizing active cases by status, flagging recently updated records, showing assigned tasks by priority, and surfacing wanted persons alerts in a persistent sidebar. The key design choice was density over whitespace: law enforcement professionals deal in data volume, and a sparse interface would have felt like it was hiding information from them.
Dashboard Design Data Density Status System Alert Hierarchy
04
Unify the search experience across all modules
Search was the number one pain point in the survey. The redesigned global search worked across all eight modules simultaneously — criminal records, cases, suspects, vehicles, and prison data — with results grouped by type and ranked by relevance. Partial name matching was added for Arabic names which have multiple common spellings. Filters were redesigned as persistent sidebar options rather than modal overlays, so officers could refine results without losing their place in the search.
Global Search Cross-module Filtering Arabic Name Matching
05
Connect related records into a unified case view
The problem of officers opening multiple tabs to view related data was solved by designing a unified case detail view. From a single screen, an officer could now see the case file, the suspect's full criminal history, any impounded vehicles connected to the case, the current prison status of involved parties, and the case update timeline — all without navigating away. Related records were linked with a clear relationship visualization rather than buried in separate modules.
Case Detail View Record Linking Relationship Visualization
06
Prototype, test, and refine with real users
High-fidelity prototypes of the dashboard, case view, and search were tested with the same group of officers from the research phase. Two rounds of testing with six users each. First round identified three critical workflow mismatches — areas where my assumptions about how officers move through a task were wrong. Second round validated the corrections and surfaced only minor labeling issues. Both rounds were conducted in Arabic, facilitated in Arabic, and analyzed with Arabic-speaking stakeholders.

05

Key Design
Decisions

Enterprise law enforcement tools have very specific tradeoffs that do not exist in consumer products. These were the decisions where getting it wrong would have had real consequences.

Decision 01 — Visual Density
Dense information over generous whitespace
Most modern UX practice pushes toward spacious, minimal interfaces. For this system, the opposite was correct. Officers need to see a lot of data at once — reducing density would mean more scrolling, more navigation, more time to reach a piece of information that might be urgent. The design uses consistent, tight spacing with strong typographic hierarchy to keep density readable, rather than reducing density to increase readability. These are not the same solution.
Decision 02 — Status Colour System
A unified colour-coded status system across all modules
Case statuses, record types, alert levels, and action states each needed a distinct visual identity that worked consistently across all eight modules. I defined six status colors — each tested for color-blind accessibility and each carrying the same meaning in every context it appeared. Red for urgent or flagged. Amber for pending action. Blue for informational. Green for resolved. Grey for archived. White for new. This replaced a system where each module had invented its own color logic independently.
Decision 03 — Change Management in the UI
Keeping familiar elements even when better alternatives existed
There were several places where I could see a significantly better UX pattern than what existed — but changing it would have broken muscle memory for officers who used the system every day under stress. The case ID field format, the navigation sidebar structure, and the record numbering convention were all kept deliberately similar to the old system, even though I disagreed with some of those choices. Trust matters more than elegance when the users are professionals who depend on the tool.
Decision 04 — Role-Based Interface Differentiation
Same system, different views based on what each role needs
Rather than building separate systems for different roles, I designed a single interface with role-based module visibility and action permissions. An investigator sees case editing and suspect linking tools. A department head sees approval queues and cross-department case summaries. An administrator sees user management and audit logs. The underlying system is the same. What is shown to each role is curated. This simplified development significantly while making each user's experience feel purpose-built for their job.

Outcomes

The measures that matter are task completion speed, error rates, and officer confidence — all of which were tracked in prototype testing and post-launch observation.

07

What I
Learned

Designing for experts is harder than designing for beginners. Experts have deep muscle memory. They will resist any change to interaction patterns they have internalized, even if those patterns are objectively worse. You have to earn the right to change things by first proving you understand how they work.
Surveys are only useful if the questions are designed correctly. The first survey draft asked officers how satisfied they were with different features. Satisfaction is a bad measure because people normalize to what they have. Reframing to "what do you do instead of using this feature" revealed the real picture. Workarounds are where the real problems hide.
Arabic RTL is not a translation layer. It is a completely different design problem. Any team that designs in English first and then "converts" to Arabic will produce an Arabic interface that feels foreign to Arabic speakers, because the spatial logic, reading rhythm, and typographic conventions of Arabic are fundamentally different from English. This needs to be built from the ground up in the target language.
Context determines what good design looks like. Dense, data-heavy interfaces are the right answer here. Minimal, whitespace-led design would be wrong for this context. There is no universal visual language for good UX — there is only the right language for the specific user doing a specific job in a specific environment.
Internal tools are underinvested in and that is a real cost. The hours officers spent navigating a broken system, the errors made from unclear data presentation, the tasks abandoned because the tool made them too hard — all of that has a cost that simply does not get measured because it is invisible. Good design on internal tools is not a nice-to-have. It is an operational decision.
