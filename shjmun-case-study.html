01

Overview

Sharjah Municipality manages everything from building permits and public health to parks, agriculture, and city infrastructure. Before 2017, residents had to physically visit offices or navigate a fragmented, outdated website that barely worked on mobile.

The organization needed more than a visual refresh. The real problem was structural: dozens of disconnected digital touchpoints, no consistent language, no unified design logic, and services built around internal processes rather than resident needs.

My work was to change that. From scratch. Across six service sectors, two languages, and three platforms — while maintaining continuity with an active system that real people depended on every day.

"A government service isn't successful when it's launched. It's successful when a resident gets what they need without needing to call anyone."

02

The
Challenge

This wasn't a single app with a clear scope. It was a living platform touching every part of a city government — each department with its own priorities, data models, and legacy systems. The design challenges were layered on top of political, technical, and cultural constraints.

120+ services, zero consistency
Every service had been built independently over many years. Different layouts, different terminology, different interaction patterns. A resident who used the building permits service had to relearn everything when they switched to health services.
Bilingual from the ground up
Arabic and English are not just translation problems — they are structurally opposite. Arabic reads right-to-left, has different typographic rules, and carries a different visual weight. Both languages had to feel native, not like one was an afterthought of the other.
A wide and unpredictable user base
Users ranged from tech-savvy young residents to elderly Arabic-speaking citizens with limited digital literacy, business owners, construction contractors, healthcare workers, and tourists. One interface had to serve all of them.
No existing design system
There was no component library, no shared token set, no documented patterns. Every new feature or service was designed from scratch by whoever was assigned to it. Scale was impossible without first building the foundation that scale runs on.
03

The
Approach



The instinct with large-scale projects is to immediately start designing. I pushed back on that. We needed to understand what we were actually dealing with before a single screen was opened in Figma.

The first three months were entirely research, audit, and strategy. Only after that did we start designing — and even then, we built the system before we built the product.

01
Audit and discovery
Mapped every existing service, touchpoint, and user flow across all six sectors. Identified 47 distinct UI patterns being used inconsistently across the platform. Ran structured interviews with 60+ residents across different demographics and Arabic-language comfort levels. Shadowed municipality staff who dealt with daily resident queries to understand where digital services were failing.
User Interviews Service Mapping Heuristic Audit Stakeholder Sessions
02
Define the information architecture
The old site organized services the way the municipality was organized internally — by department. Residents don't think in departments. They think in tasks: "I need a building permit," not "I need to visit the Urban Planning department." We restructured the entire IA around resident intent, not organizational hierarchy. This alone reduced navigation depth by an average of 2.3 levels across services.
Card Sorting Tree Testing IA Mapping Journey Mapping
03
Build the bilingual design system first
Before designing a single service flow, we built the system that all services would run on. A bilingual component library with 180+ components, each tested in both Arabic RTL and English LTR. Type scales, color tokens, spacing rules, form patterns, feedback states — documented so clearly that a junior developer or a new designer could build a new service without needing to invent anything. This was the highest-leverage work of the entire project.
Figma Design System Token Architecture RTL / LTR Components Documentation
04
Design iteratively, test continuously
Services were redesigned sector by sector, starting with the highest-volume ones: building permits, health certifications, and environment licenses. Each went through three rounds of usability testing with real residents before going to development. We specifically recruited low-digital-literacy users for testing — if they could complete the task, anyone could.
Usability Testing Prototype Testing A/B Testing Accessibility Review
05
Rollout and live iteration
We launched sectors in phases rather than all at once. This let us absorb real-world feedback early and apply it to later phases. Post-launch analytics and call center data were our ongoing feedback loop — every spike in support calls pointed to something we needed to fix in the UI.

04

Key Design
Decisions

These were the decisions where we had real choices and real tradeoffs — not just styling, but structural calls that shaped how the whole platform behaves.

Decision 01 — Navigation Structure
Organizing by resident intent, not by internal department
The old structure mirrored the municipality org chart. We rebuilt it entirely around what residents are trying to accomplish.
Before
"Urban Planning Department → Permits Section → Building Permits → New Application" — four levels deep, department-first language
After
"Building Services → Apply for a Building Permit" — two levels, task-first language, same result reached faster
Decision 02 — Bilingual Architecture
Treating Arabic as a first-class language, not a translation
Most bilingual government sites in the region treat one language as primary and the other as a port. We designed every component simultaneously in both directions from day one — different type scales, different line heights, different icon placement rules, different form input behaviors. This added significant time to the design system build but meant both experiences genuinely worked rather than one of them merely existing.
Decision 03 — Progressive Disclosure in Forms
Showing only what the resident needs at each step
Government forms are notoriously long. The temptation is to put everything on one page to "show transparency." Testing showed this caused abandonment within the first 30 seconds. We moved to a step-by-step model where each screen has one clear question or task, with context shown only when relevant.
Before
Single-page forms with 20+ fields visible at once. Average abandonment at field 4. Users called the call center to complete applications.
After
Multi-step flows, never more than 5 fields per screen. Clear progress indication. Completion rate improved significantly across tested services.
Decision 04 — Accessibility as a constraint, not a feature
Building for the full range of residents, not the average one
Accessibility was treated as a design constraint from the beginning, not a checklist at the end. Every component was built to WCAG 2.1 AA as a baseline. Color contrast ratios, touch target sizes, keyboard navigation, and screen reader compatibility were tested in every sprint — not saved for a final accessibility pass that often gets cut when timelines slip.
05

Design
System

The design system was the most consequential deliverable of this project. Not the website. The system.

A well-designed page can still create chaos if the next designer builds the next page differently. The system was what allowed consistency to scale across 120+ services without requiring my involvement in every decision.

Bilingual component library — Arabic RTL and English LTR running from the same token set

What the system included: a full token architecture (color, typography, spacing, elevation), 180+ components each documented with usage rules and bilingual variants, interaction patterns for forms, modals, navigation, tables, and data display, error and empty state patterns, and a governance model for how new components get proposed and added.

What it enabled: a team of four designers and eight developers could build new services in days rather than weeks, with near-zero QA time spent on visual inconsistencies. When a department needed a new form type or a new service category, they could assemble it from existing patterns rather than designing from scratch.

A design system isn't valuable when you build it. It's valuable six months later when someone else uses it to build something you weren't involved in — and it's still right.

07

What I
Learned

Eight years of continuous work on a single platform at this scale teaches you things you cannot learn from shorter projects.

Systems thinking is the most underrated design skill. The ability to design a component that works today and also works correctly in six unforeseen contexts — that is more valuable than the ability to produce a beautiful single screen.
Stakeholder alignment is part of the design work. A technically perfect design that nobody internally believes in will not survive. I learned to spend real time helping department heads understand what we were doing and why — not just presenting designs, but building conviction.
Complexity does not require complexity. The hardest design challenge on this project was making 120 different government services feel simple and similar. The temptation is to over-customize each one. The discipline is to find the pattern underneath them all and design that.
Bilingual design needs to start on day one. Any team that designs in one language and "adds" the other later will ship a product where one language is clearly an afterthought. Typography, layout, iconography, form behavior — all of it changes depending on reading direction, and those changes need to be designed, not retrofitted.
The real user test is call center volume. Resident satisfaction surveys can be gamed. Support call volume cannot. Every spike in calls after a release told us something specific had failed — and that feedback loop, over years, made the design measurably better.
