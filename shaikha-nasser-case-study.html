01

Overview

In April 2025, Shaikha Nasser Al Nowais was running for Secretary-General of UN Tourism — one of the most prominent international positions in global tourism governance. Her campaign needed a website. They came to me with four weeks until the election.

This was a category of work I had never done before. Campaign websites for international elections at UN level are a very specific thing. The audience is not citizens scrolling social media — it is tourism ministers, government delegations, international press, and UN member state representatives. Every word and every design choice would be seen by people deciding whether to back a candidate.

I had no brief template to follow, no reference from previous work, and no senior designer to check with. I was the only designer on the project. So I did what you do when you do not have experience: I went and got it, fast. Research, decisions, design — all in parallel, all under a hard deadline that had no flexibility.

The deadline was the election. There is no version of "we need a few more days" when a candidate is on a ballot.

The candidate
Shaikha Nasser Al Nowais, UAE — running for Secretary-General of UN Tourism, the global body responsible for responsible and sustainable tourism development
The audience
International government delegates, UN member state representatives, global tourism ministers, international press, and the public across five language regions
What was needed
A campaign website that communicated credibility, vision, and international readiness — in five languages, built from scratch, live before the vote
02

The
Challenge

Three things made this project harder than any other website I had designed. The category was new to me. The audience was unusually high-stakes. And the clock was already running when the brief arrived.

An audience I had never designed for
UN delegates and government ministers assess credibility differently from consumers. The design could not look like a startup landing page or a corporate brochure. It needed to project institutional seriousness without being cold, and warmth without being informal. That balance required research I had to do from scratch.
Five languages with real typographic differences
Arabic reads right to left. French, Portuguese, and Spanish use diacritics that affect line spacing and text rendering. Each language version had to feel native and professionally produced, not like a machine translation dropped into the same layout. The design had to hold up structurally across all five.
Four weeks from zero to live
Research, concept, design, content integration, multilingual testing, and launch — all inside a month. There was no room for multiple design directions or extended feedback rounds. The first direction had to be the right one, which meant the research phase had to be sharp enough to eliminate wrong directions before a single screen was opened.
No comparable UAE precedent to reference
Previous UAE candidates running for international UN-level positions had either no web presence or weak ones. There was no local example to learn from. I had to look at how other national campaigns for international bodies were designed globally and then adapt that understanding to this specific candidate, context, and audience.
03

Research
Process

When you have never designed something before, the first thing you do is go find people who have and study what worked. I spent the first days entirely in research — not designing, not even sketching, just learning the category.

01
Study comparable international campaigns
I looked at campaign websites and digital presences for candidates who had run for senior international roles at the UN, UNESCO, WHO, and similar bodies. I was looking for patterns in how credibility was communicated visually, how vision content was structured, and how multilingual experiences were handled without one language feeling primary. The consistent pattern across successful campaigns was a tone that was authoritative but human — formal enough for institutional audiences, personal enough to build emotional connection.
Competitive Benchmarking Category Research Visual Pattern Analysis
02
Understand the candidate's positioning
Before designing anything I needed to understand what Shaikha Nasser stood for, what made her candidacy distinct, and what the campaign wanted international audiences to feel when they arrived at the site. I worked through the brief content carefully — her background in sustainable tourism, her record in the UAE, her vision for UN Tourism's direction. These became the design inputs. The colour system, the typographic tone, the content hierarchy — all of it came from understanding the candidate's positioning first.
Brief Analysis Candidate Positioning Audience Mapping
03
Map the multilingual requirements
Arabic was already familiar territory. For French, Portuguese, and Spanish I researched the typographic norms, character sets, and average text length expansion relative to English. French and Spanish text typically runs 15 to 25% longer than English. Portuguese can run up to 30% longer in certain constructions. Every layout had to absorb this expansion without breaking. I tested critical UI elements — navigation labels, call to action buttons, section headings — in all five languages before finalising any layout decisions.

04

Site
Structure

Campaign websites for international candidates have a very specific information job to do. A UN delegate visiting the site needs to answer three questions fast: who is this person, what do they stand for, and why should we back them. Every section of the site was designed to answer one of those questions.

Hero
First impression
Who she is, what position she is running for, and the core campaign message — communicated in under 5 seconds. Optimised for the international press and delegate who arrives knowing nothing.
Biography
Credibility and track record
Her career, achievements, and relevant experience in tourism governance — structured to read quickly with key milestones surfaced prominently rather than buried in a paragraph.
Vision
The platform
What she will do as Secretary-General. Her policy positions and priorities for UN Tourism. This section needed to feel substantive and specific, not generic aspiration.
Endorsements
Third-party credibility
Support from governments, tourism bodies, and international figures. One of the most-read sections by delegates who are assessing which candidate has the backing to be effective.
Media
Press, interviews, video
News articles and video clips — a resource centre for journalists and researchers, and a signal of international media presence to delegates assessing profile.
News
Campaign updates
A live section updated throughout the campaign period with events, statements, and coverage — giving repeat visitors a reason to return and signalling an active, well-organised campaign.
Contact
Direct reach
A clean contact section for press, delegates, and government representatives to reach the campaign directly. Kept simple — the audience expects professionalism, not a complex form.

The multilingual switcher was placed prominently in the navigation on every page. Switching languages preserved the user's current position within the site rather than returning them to the homepage — an important detail for a UN delegate who might want to re-read a section of the Vision page in their native language.

05

Design
Decisions

The first draft was approved without revisions. That does not happen by accident. It happens when research is thorough enough that the design decisions are already justified before anyone sees the screens. These were the decisions that mattered most.

Decision 01 — Visual Tone
Institutional authority, not corporate polish
A campaign website for a UN candidacy sits in a very specific position between government formality and human warmth. I chose a typographic-led design with generous white space, a restrained colour palette anchored in deep navy and warm gold, and photography given prominent space rather than being used as decoration. The result reads as serious without being cold. It signals that the candidate is internationally minded — not a regional official with a website, but someone already operating at that level of the world.
Decision 02 — Colour System
A palette that worked across all five language contexts
Color carries cultural meaning and that meaning shifts across audiences. The palette I chose had to work for Arabic-speaking audiences in the Gulf, French-speaking audiences in Africa and Europe, Spanish and Portuguese audiences in Latin America, and English-speaking audiences globally. Deep navy for trust and authority, warm gold for the UAE identity and international distinction, and clean white for clarity. Each of those associations holds across the target cultural contexts without carrying conflicting meaning.
Decision 03 — Arabic as Equal, Not Secondary
The Arabic version was designed in parallel, not translated after
Because Arabic and RTL layout was already part of my daily work at Sharjah Municipality, I designed the Arabic version simultaneously with English rather than designing English first and then adapting. This meant the Arabic experience did not feel like a translation — the typographic scale was adjusted for Arabic character proportions, the layout rhythm was rebuilt for right-to-left reading, and the navigation structure was verified in Arabic before being finalized. For a UAE candidate addressing Arab-speaking member states, this mattered.
Decision 04 — Content Hierarchy on the Vision Page
Specific commitments over broad aspiration
Campaign vision pages typically fill space with aspirational language that says nothing specific. UN delegates have seen hundreds of these. I pushed for the vision section to open with concrete priority areas stated clearly, with the broader context supporting them rather than leading. This made the page readable in 60 seconds by someone who was skimming, while still providing depth for anyone who wanted it. The structure was: clear statement, brief explanation, implication for UN Tourism's direction — repeated per priority area.
Decision 05 — Why the First Draft Was Approved
Research reduced the variables before the design started
The first draft being approved without revisions came down to one thing: by the time I opened Figma, there were very few genuine unknowns left. The research had already answered the questions of visual tone, content priority, colour system, and typographic approach. The design was not a creative proposal — it was the execution of conclusions I had already reached through research. When a client sees a first draft that is clearly not guesswork, they feel it immediately. That is what happened.

What I
Learned

Not knowing a category is not the same as being unqualified for it. I had never designed a campaign website before. That gap was closed in days through focused research. The design skills transferred completely. What you need when entering a new category is not prior experience in that exact category — it is a fast, honest research process and the discipline to let what you find actually change your decisions.
First drafts get approved when the brief is fully understood first. The client did not have to ask for revisions because there was nothing to revise — the design was already the answer to the right question. Most revision cycles happen because the designer started designing before they finished understanding. Research is not a phase before the real work. It is the most important part of the real work.
Multilingual design is a structural problem, not a content problem. The challenge of five languages was not translation — the client handled that. The challenge was building layouts and typographic systems that held together when the same content was 30% longer in Portuguese than in English, and flipped entirely in Arabic. That requires solving for the hardest case first and then testing everything else against it.
Hard deadlines make you a better designer. A four-week timeline with no flexibility eliminates a specific category of bad habit: endless exploration, unnecessary iterations, and decision avoidance dressed up as thoroughness. When the deadline is fixed, you become very honest very quickly about what is actually a meaningful design decision and what is just procrastination.
The work you do in new categories compounds. Before this project, I had never designed for a political or international campaign audience. Now I have, and the thinking from that experience informs how I approach any project involving institutional credibility, multilingual global audiences, or high-stakes one-shot deadlines. New categories do not just add to your portfolio. They add to how you think.
